Featured case1973Supreme Court of India

Ram Narain v. State of Uttar Pradesh

Handwriting / questioned documents

The Supreme Court considered the evidentiary value of handwriting-expert opinion, the role of specimen material and the court’s task in evaluating disputed writings alongside all the other evidence on record.

Case at a Glance

Two anonymous ransom letters made disputed handwriting central to a criminal appeal that reached the Supreme Court.

The Story

The Supreme Court’s judgment records that five-year-old Mannu went missing in Kanpur in August 1964. A postcard and, later, an inland letter demanded ransom for his return. The child was recovered in January 1965. The prosecution alleged that Ram Narain had written the two anonymous letters; he denied authorship.

The conviction under review was for attempted extortion and rested on the conclusion that Ram Narain wrote the letters. A handwriting expert gave evidence for the prosecution. The trial court, Sessions Court and High Court accepted the opinion after examining the writings themselves. The appeal therefore raised a focused question about how much weight expert opinion and a court’s own comparison may carry.

The Forensic Connection

The questioned items were the two ransom letters; comparison material included writings attributed to Ram Narain. The Court explained that handwriting-expert opinion is relevant but fallible and must be treated cautiously. A court may compare writings to assess the expert’s reasoning, not to assume the role of a technical examiner. In this case, the lower courts’ comparisons and the expert evidence were considered together.

The Key Question

Could the handwriting evidence, assessed with the courts’ own comparison and the record, support the finding that Ram Narain wrote the letters?

Why This Case Matters

It illustrates both caution and judicial evaluation: expert opinion is not automatically conclusive, but the Court did not treat it as unusable. The decision turns on the record and how the courts assessed it, not on a general rule that handwriting evidence alone always proves authorship.

Sukhram v. State of Himachal Pradesh

Specimen handwriting / expert evidence

The judgment underscores the importance of suitable specimen material and the limits of relying on a handwriting opinion without proper evidentiary procedure.

Case at a Glance

A specimen-writing question also raises a procedural one: when could investigators obtain comparison material, and which rules applied at the time?

The Story

The case is identified as a 2016 Supreme Court decision in Sukhram v. State of Himachal Pradesh. Its relevance to questioned-document practice is commonly discussed through the rules for obtaining specimen handwriting or signatures. The date matters because Section 311A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which provides a procedure for directing a person to provide specimen signatures or handwriting, came into force in 2006.

A later Delhi trial-court judgment cites Sukhram for the proposition that Section 311A operates prospectively. This helps frame the legal issue, but it does not by itself establish what particular writings were examined in Sukhram, what an examiner concluded, or that a disputed authorship finding was made. Those case-specific details should be taken from the Supreme Court’s own record, not inferred from a later citation.

The Forensic Connection

Specimen or known material is the basis against which questioned writing may be compared. Its identity, provenance, date, manner of collection and suitability are distinct matters from the later evaluation of an expert’s opinion. A procedural ruling on when a specimen was lawfully obtained is not a scientific conclusion about whether two writings share an author.

The Key Question

Which procedure applied to obtaining specimen handwriting or signatures at the relevant time, and what can that procedural ruling establish?

Why This Case Matters

It encourages readers to separate the legal authority to obtain a sample from the scientific adequacy of the comparison. The present page does not claim a case-specific expert conclusion beyond what the cited later decision reports.

2015High Court / Full Bench

Bande Siva Shankara Srinivasa Prasad v. Ravi Surya Prakash Babu & Others

Signature examination / contemporaneity

The case addressed whether a disputed signature could be referred for expert examination when the available admitted signatures were from a substantially different period.

Case at a Glance

A disputed signature, a substantial time gap, and a technical question sent by a Full Bench to the Central Forensic Science Laboratory.

The Story

This 2015 Full Bench decision of the Andhra Pradesh High Court arose from connected civil revision petitions. The reference exposed inconsistent approaches in lower-court decisions: some treated a long interval between a disputed signature and admitted comparison signatures as a reason not to seek expert help; others considered expert comparison possible despite the gap.

The Full Bench asked whether contemporaneity was an essential precondition to sending questioned handwriting or signatures for expert opinion. Rather than answer a scientific question by assumption, the Court sought a report from the Central Forensic Science Laboratory in Hyderabad on when contemporaneous standards are normally helpful, what “contemporaneous” means, and whether comparison is possible without such material.

The Forensic Connection

The CFSL report described contemporaneous writings as preferred for better evaluation, but not invariably necessary. It explained that a meaningful opinion depends on the available data, comparable writing features, natural variation and the circumstances of the questioned material. The legal issue—whether a civil court may refer signatures for examination—is separate from the examiner’s scientific assessment of whether the supplied standards support a conclusion.

The Key Question

Does a time gap automatically prevent expert comparison, or does suitability depend on the material and circumstances?

Why This Case Matters

The decision resists a simplistic “same period or no examination” rule. It shows why an examiner may need to evaluate the age, provenance and quality of each standard—and may need to qualify or decline an opinion when the available comparison material is inadequate.

Fingerprint Evidence in Indian Criminal Proceedings

Latent print / identification / expert evidence

Indian courts often assess fingerprint evidence in the context of other material on record, with special emphasis on the forensic methodology, chain of custody and independent verification.

Case at a Glance

This entry is a general overview, not a single named court case: it introduces how Indian proceedings may assess latent-print evidence and its context.

The Story

Unlike the handwriting and signature entries above, this card does not identify one prosecution, judgment, accused, or crime-scene impression. It summarizes a forensic issue that arises across cases: whether a latent print recovered from an object or scene can be meaningfully compared with a known impression, and how that comparison fits with the rest of the evidence.

For a concrete further-reading example, the Bombay High Court’s 2015 decision in Sanjay @ Papdya @ Pawan @ Prashant @ Rahul and Others v. State of Maharashtra discusses a palm impression on an almirah in a dacoity investigation. That judgment is an illustration, not the case behind this general card, and its facts should not be substituted for an unspecified case.

The Forensic Connection

Examiners assess the quality and detail of a latent impression, its collection and preservation, the comparison material and the basis for any identification. A court also considers how the print was found, documented and linked to the alleged activity. Presence of a print can be relevant, but interpretation depends on circumstances; it does not by itself establish when or why a person touched an object.

The Key Question

What does the impression and its context support—and what limits remain on the inference from a print identification?

Why Read This Overview?

It offers a framework for considering impression quality, collection, comparison and context together, while distinguishing a general forensic discussion from the facts and findings of any one case.

What These Cases Teach Us

1. Comparison material matters

Suitable known writing or signatures are fundamental to a meaningful comparison. Weak or unsuitable exemplars reduce the reliability of the conclusion.

2. Expert opinion is evidence, not an automatic conclusion

Courts evaluate expert evidence along with the complete record rather than treating a forensic opinion as a direct verdict.

3. Document provenance matters

Where a document came from and how it was obtained can be as important as the visible writing features themselves.

4. Scientific examination requires objectivity

A professional examiner must assess both similarities and differences instead of searching only for confirming features.

5. Limitations must be communicated

The strength and limits of the examination should be clearly explained so that the evidentiary value is understood in context.

6. Forensic evidence belongs in context

Document and fingerprint evidence should be interpreted alongside circumstances, corroboration and the broader case record.
