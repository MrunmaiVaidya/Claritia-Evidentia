Questioned Documents & Handwriting

France1894–1906

The Dreyfus Affair

Handwriting examination · disputed authorship

A contested handwriting comparison of a military memorandum became part of a notorious wrongful-conviction case.

Background
In 1894, French army officer Alfred Dreyfus was accused of passing military information to Germany. The case centered in part on an anonymous memorandum known as the bordereau.
Forensic question
Could the handwriting on the bordereau be attributed to Dreyfus?
Evidence examined
The bordereau and handwriting specimens used by contemporary examiners; the prosecution also relied on a secret dossier that was not disclosed to the defense.
Examination / methodology
Military handwriting experts compared writing characteristics. Their opinions were divided; this historical examination predated modern questioned-document practice and should not be equated with current standards.
Key findings
The handwriting opinions were contested rather than a unanimous or conclusive identification. Later scrutiny focused on other potential authors, including Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy.
Role of forensic evidence
The handwriting claim helped implicate Dreyfus, but it was one part of a case also driven by undisclosed evidence and institutional decisions; it did not independently establish guilt.
Outcome / significance
Dreyfus was convicted, later pardoned, and exonerated and reinstated in 1906. The affair remains a caution about disputed expertise, disclosure, and confirmation bias.

Forensic Insight: A handwriting opinion must be presented with its limitations and alternatives. Independent review, transparent reasoning, and access to relevant evidence matter.

Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Dreyfus affair”.

United States1932–1936

The Lindbergh Kidnapping

Ransom-note handwriting · questioned documents

Handwriting comparison of ransom notes was presented alongside other evidence in the investigation of the kidnapping of Charles Lindbergh’s son.

Background
Charles Lindbergh’s infant son was kidnapped from the family home in New Jersey in 1932. A series of ransom notes and communications followed.
Forensic question
Did the ransom-note handwriting correspond to writing associated with suspect Bruno Richard Hauptmann?
Evidence examined
Ransom notes and handwriting exemplars attributed to Hauptmann. The prosecution also presented other evidence, including evidence concerning ransom money and the child’s remains.
Examination / methodology
Handwriting experts compared writing in the notes with known and requested samples. Their comparison was presented as expert testimony at trial.
Key findings
Prosecution experts testified that the notes were written by Hauptmann. The identification was expert opinion, not an instrument-based certainty, and was part of a wider evidentiary case.
Role of forensic evidence
The ransom-note comparison linked the suspect to communications in the case; it should not be described as the sole basis for the verdict.
Outcome / significance
Hauptmann was convicted and executed in 1936. The case is frequently discussed in the history of forensic document examination and the presentation of expert testimony.

Forensic Insight: Separate observable similarities from an authorship conclusion, explain the comparison material and its limitations, and consider the opinion within the full case context.

Sources: Federal Bureau of Investigation, “Lindbergh Kidnapping”.

West Germany1983

The Hitler Diaries

Document authentication · handwriting · materials analysis

A set of purported diaries attributed to Adolf Hitler was exposed as forged after scrutiny of the documents and their materials.

Background
In 1983, a West German magazine announced the discovery of diaries represented as Adolf Hitler’s personal writings. They were published before their authenticity was adequately established.
Forensic question
Were the manuscripts genuine historical documents from Hitler’s period, and were they written by him?
Evidence examined
The diaries’ handwriting and physical materials, including paper and ink, were examined.
Examination / methodology
Examination combined handwriting assessment with scientific testing of the document materials and checks against historical details. Testing indicated postwar materials, inconsistent with the claimed provenance.
Key findings
The diaries were modern forgeries, later attributed to Konrad Kujau. The materials evidence contradicted the claim that they were wartime originals.
Role of forensic evidence
Scientific material analysis was decisive in disproving authenticity; handwriting resemblance or an initial expert endorsement could not override contradictory physical evidence.
Outcome / significance
The publication was withdrawn and the forgery became a major example of failed document authentication and inadequate source verification.

Forensic Insight: Authenticate the whole document. Handwriting must be assessed alongside paper, ink, provenance, chronology, and independent historical evidence.

Sources: TIME, “Hitler’s Forged Diaries”; Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Hitler Diaries”.

Argentina1892

The Francisca Rojas Case

Latent fingerprint identification

A bloody fingerprint in a double-murder investigation is widely cited as an early use of fingerprints to identify a suspect.

Background
In Necochea, Argentina, Francisca Rojas’s two children were murdered. Rojas accused a neighbor; the investigation turned to a bloody impression at the scene.
Forensic question
Whose finger made the bloody impression, and could it help identify the person responsible?
Evidence examined
A bloody fingerprint associated with the scene and inked comparison prints. Historical accounts credit police official Eduardo Álvarez and the classification work of Juan Vucetich.
Examination / methodology
The impression was compared with recorded fingerprints using Vucetich’s developing classification and comparison system, an early method in the history of fingerprint identification.
Key findings
Accounts report that the print was identified as Rojas’s; she later confessed. The case is often called the first murder solved or first conviction through fingerprint evidence, though those descriptions and the exact legal role of the print are not always consistently reported.
Role of forensic evidence
The fingerprint helped redirect suspicion toward Rojas and challenge her accusation. It should not be inflated into a claim that the print alone established every fact of the crime.
Outcome / significance
The case is a landmark in the adoption of fingerprints for criminal identification, particularly in Argentina and the history of forensic identification.

Forensic Insight: Preserve historical nuance: distinguish identification, confession, prosecution, and conviction, and document the comparison basis rather than repeating “first” claims without qualification.

Sources: U.S. National Institute of Justice, The Fingerprint Sourcebook; Simon A. Cole, Suspect Identities, Harvard University Press.

United States1903, commonly dated

The Will West / William West Story

Fingerprint identification · anthropometry

A widely repeated prison-identification story contrasts two men’s similar appearance and Bertillon measurements with different fingerprints.

Background
The story concerns two Leavenworth prisoners, commonly identified as Will West and William West, whose names and physical measurements were reportedly very similar.
Forensic question
Could an identification system distinguish the two records when names, appearance, and anthropometric measurements were alike?
Evidence examined
Reported Bertillon measurements and the two men’s fingerprint records.
Examination / methodology
The often-recounted account says the measurements were compared and fingerprints then used to distinguish the records. Later histories caution that popular versions simplify or vary in their details.
Key findings
The fingerprint records were different, despite the reported similarity in measurements and appearance.
Role of forensic evidence
The episode became an illustration of fingerprints as a more discriminating identification method than the particular anthropometric comparison in this anecdote—not proof that fingerprint identification is infallible.
Outcome / significance
The story is associated with the transition toward fingerprint-based identification in institutions. Its details should be treated as a historical anecdote, not a fully documented modern case study.

Forensic Insight: No identification method is error-proof. Use suitable comparison material, quality controls, independent verification, and careful record management.

Sources: U.S. National Institute of Justice, The Fingerprint Sourcebook; Simon A. Cole, Suspect Identities, Harvard University Press.

United States / Spain2004

The Brandon Mayfield Misidentification

Latent print comparison · verification and quality control

The FBI withdrew its identification of an Oregon attorney after Spanish authorities disagreed about a latent print associated with the Madrid train bombings.

Background
After the March 2004 Madrid train bombings, investigators examined a latent print on a bag associated with bomb-making materials. Oregon attorney Brandon Mayfield, a U.S. citizen, became a candidate in the FBI comparison.
Forensic question
Did the latent print originate from Mayfield?
Evidence examined
A partial latent fingerprint and reference fingerprint records, including Mayfield’s prints and material available to Spanish authorities.
Examination / methodology
FBI examiners made an identification, which underwent internal review. Spanish examiners disagreed; further investigation and review led the FBI to withdraw its identification. The U.S. Department of Justice Inspector General later examined the FBI’s handling of the matter.
Key findings
The FBI’s identification of Mayfield was erroneous. The Inspector General identified process and judgment failures, including contextual influences and deficiencies in verification and review.
Role of forensic evidence
The fingerprint association drove investigative attention and Mayfield’s arrest and detention. He was not convicted; the FBI acknowledged the error and apologized.
Outcome / significance
Mayfield was released after the misidentification was corrected. The case prompted scrutiny of latent-print examination practices, independent verification, contextual bias, and quality assurance.

Forensic Insight: Examiners need context management, documented reasoning, independent verification, and a willingness to revisit an identification when conflicting evidence emerges.

Sources: U.S. Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General, A Review of the FBI’s Handling of the Brandon Mayfield Case.
